How Malware Actually Works

Malware isn't just one single thing. It comes in different forms, each with its own way of getting into devices and causing trouble. Here is a clear breakdown of the four main types you should know about.

Needs a host program

Computer Viruses

A virus is malicious code that hitches a ride on a legitimate file or executable program. It sits quiet until you actually open that file or run the installer. Once activated, it copies itself into other programs on your computer and can spread to other devices when those files are shared.

How it reaches your computer

  • Suspicious email attachments disguised as invoices or office documents.
  • Pirated software, cracked games, or unofficial media downloads.
  • Phishing links sent over messaging apps or SMS.
  • Plugging in unknown USB drives carrying infected files.

What happens when infected

  • Your system slows down significantly, freezes, or crashes repeatedly.
  • Personal photos, documents, or operating system files get corrupted or deleted.
  • Attackers capture sensitive information like logins and passwords.
  • Your device gets hijacked to silently send spam emails to others.
Real Example: The ILOVEYOU Virus (2000)

This virus arrived in millions of inboxes disguised as a harmless text attachment named LOVE-LETTER-FOR-YOU.TXT.vbs. When users opened it, the script wiped personal files and automatically emailed copies of itself to every contact in the user's Outlook address book.

Self-spreading

Computer Worms

Worms are much more aggressive than regular viruses because they don't need human help or a host file to spread. Once a worm finds a single vulnerable computer on a network, it scans for other connected systems and infects them automatically.

How it spreads

Worms exploit unpatched security bugs in operating systems and network software. They can also travel across shared Wi-Fi networks and plugged-in USB storage.

Network impact

Because worms rapidly replicate across networks, they consume massive amounts of bandwidth. This often crashes local networks, slows internet traffic, and opens hidden backdoors.

Best protection

Turn on automatic operating system updates. Security patches fix the specific software vulnerabilities that worms rely on to jump between devices.

Deceptive disguise

Trojan Horses

Named after the famous Greek story, a Trojan disguises itself as something useful or harmless—like an utility app, a software update, or a free game download. When you install it, it secretly carries out malicious tasks in the background.

Common disguised forms

Fake media players asking for an update, free utility software from untrusted websites, cracked versions of paid games, or fake PDF attachments in emails.

What attackers gain

Trojans create secret access points (backdoors) for hackers. They log what you type on your keyboard, access your webcam, steal banking logins, or drop additional viruses onto your machine.

Common Scenario

You download a installer for a game you didn't want to pay for. The game opens and works fine, but in the background, a hidden Trojan script logs into your web browser to extract saved passwords and cookies.

Data extortion

Ransomware

Ransomware takes your personal files or entire device hostage by scrambling the data with strong encryption. It then displays a message demanding money (usually in cryptocurrency like Bitcoin) in exchange for the key to unlock your files.

Crypto Ransomware

Encrypts specific photos, documents, and databases so you can't open them.

Locker Ransomware

Completely locks your screen or operating system so you can't even log in.

Double Extortion

Attackers steal sensitive files first, threatening to leak them online if you don't pay.

Prevention Tip

Keep regular offline backups of important files so you never have to consider paying a ransom.
